Context

Kiln is institutional yield infrastructure with $18B+ in assets staked across 200+ institutions, from exchanges to fintechs to funds. Its rails let those institutions earn onchain yield without ever giving up custody of their assets.

That earning happens two ways, both specific to crypto:

  • Staking → locking up assets to help run a blockchain, which pays rewards in return.
  • DeFi → lending assets out through automated lending markets that pay interest.

Here's the catch for a designer: almost none of that infrastructure has a screen. Most clients plug into it through code.

Only one surface is built for actual people: the Dashboard, where a client's treasury, finance and ops teams log in to see, operate and report on their positions.

I came in as Kiln was moving from staking into DeFi, right when a staking-only screen stopped being enough.

The problem

Every institution we spoke to was quietly answering the same question:

"Where do all our yield positions actually stand right now?"

No product answered it. So teams rebuilt the answer by hand: exporting from protocol interfaces, reading half-complete custodian dashboards, and stitching it together in a spreadsheet a finance analyst refreshed manually, often from a CSV another team had dropped in Slack.

The question was hard for structural reasons: yield lived across many protocols, each with its own interface; staking and DeFi are mechanically different primitives, so nothing lined them up in one view; and reporting deep enough for compliance and accounting simply didn't exist in-product.

A missing feature is annoying. A missing source of truth is expensive: it costs headcount, and it makes people distrust their own numbers.

What the status quo cost

~half a day per report, reconciling positions by hand in a spreadsheet
Every change routed through our implementation team, a constant queue
~2 days to onboard and reconcile a new set of positions

Objective

Take the Dashboard from a staking-only admin tool to a unified yield portfolio platform: one place for institutions to discover, deploy, monitor and report on every position, staking and DeFi, across 20+ assets, held through the custodians they already use.

My role

  • I was the founding and sole product designer on the Dashboard.
  • I led product direction, UX, UI, and the design system end to end.
  • I built Kiln's first design system in Figma on top of shadcn/Radix, implemented in code by engineering.
  • I prototyped and shipped some features directly in code, including their empty and error states.
  • I partnered weekly with product, frontend and data engineering, and kept clients close throughout.

Research

Before designing anything, I needed to understand how institutions actually operate yield day to day, and why every team ends up rebuilding the same spreadsheet. I worked four angles:

User interviews

Sessions with client-side treasury, ops and finance teams. I also worked with our own account managers and customer success, who absorbed the pain daily, to map workflows and frustrations.

Workflow audit

How teams tracked positions today: protocol UIs, custodian dashboards, hand-built spreadsheets, CSVs passed over Slack by our own finance team.

Prospect signals

Institutions sitting on idle stablecoins and Bitcoin, looking for a compliant way to put them to work, and teams already in DeFi hitting a wall at every step.

Custodian & competitive landscape

What custodians actually expose, often partial or delayed, and where the reachable yield set silently shrinks.

Five structural pain points showed up across funds, exchanges, fintechs and treasuries:

  1. 01

    Fragmented access

    A different interface and workflow per protocol. A UX problem and a security problem at once.

  2. 02

    Limited custodial integrations

    Custodians expose incomplete or delayed access, shrinking the options actually reachable.

  3. 03

    Insufficient reporting

    No provider gave finance and compliance the depth they needed, so teams built their own reconciliation: slow, costly, error-prone.

  4. 04

    Blind to risk

    No tooling for cross-protocol risk; evaluating a vault took a DeFi specialist most teams didn't have.

  5. 05

    No way to scale

    Balancing across protocols was manual, so growing exposure meant growing headcount.

Testing: I shipped to design partners and watched

Because the platform was rolling out to design partners, I didn't test in a lab, I tested in production. Each client had a dedicated Slack channel that became a continuous feedback loop: ship a slice, watch how they used it, gather reactions, iterate. I paired that with Mixpanel to see which views people actually opened and which they ignored, and every change a client asked for had to justify its business impact before it entered the backlog, which kept the roadmap honest.

Two decisions the feedback changed:

"All accounts" became the default view. A team doesn't manage one account, it manages a dozen sub-accounts across custodians. The first version made you pick an account first; Mixpanel showed most sessions went straight to "all accounts", and clients said the per-account default added a click before the answer they actually wanted: the total. So the aggregate view became the landing state.

Historical went from a side tab to a first-class view. The first build led with a real-time snapshot. Finance teams told us, repeatedly in the Slack channels, that a snapshot they couldn't reproduce was useless for reporting: they needed to answer "what did we hold on this date" for an auditor. So the historical view became half the product.

Design decision & screens

Staking and DeFi don't behave alike: different lifecycles, lock-ups, reward mechanics. The obvious move was two products, a staking view and a DeFi view side by side. I killed that early: it would have meant learning the tool twice, two reporting grammars to reconcile at audit time, and no way to answer the one question that mattered, what's our total yield across everything.

So I made the opposite bet: one portfolio view, and three numbers that mean the same thing everywhere. You learn the product once. Under the hood the primitives stay different; on screen, they finally line up.

Three other paths I ruled out

One big table, like the spreadsheet it replaced

Tempting because it's familiar, zero learning curve. But it doesn't scale across 20+ assets, two yield types and several custodians; it hides the staking-vs-DeFi split and the time dimension; and it just rebuilds the spreadsheet pain in-product. Ruled out.

Navigate by custodian or protocol first

Tempting because it matches "where are my assets held". But teams think in total yield and risk, not "my positions at custodian X"; leading with custodian re-fragments the view and buries the portfolio answer. It became a filter, not the top-level structure.

Real-time only, skip the historical view

Tempting because it's far less data engineering and ships faster. But compliance and audit need a defensible record of what was held on a given date, one of the core reasons the product exists. I built the historical view anyway.

Portfolio overview → Total balance and total interest, split staking vs DeFi, then every position in a single table: asset, chains, type, share, balance, interest.

Real-time to operate, historical to answer for

Total balance & total interest earned, historical view → Allocation and interest over any window, as a defensible record, not a screenshot reconstructed after the fact.

Down to the position

Down to the position → Per-account performance and full history, so a team can drill from the portfolio all the way to a single stake and still answer for it.

The constraints I designed inside

None of this happened on a blank canvas. Three constraints shaped every screen.

A design system I had to build first

Kiln had no design system when I arrived. I built its first one in Figma on top of shadcn/Radix, and engineering implemented it in code. That was a constraint and a lever at once: every screen had to fit the system, but because I work in code too, I could extend a component or ship a change myself through the Figma-to-code pipeline, without waiting on a hand-off.

Compliance and custody

This is regulated financial infrastructure: institutions never give up custody, and every number on screen can end up in front of an auditor. The product could never overstate or infer; it had to show sourced, defensible data and a reproducible history.

Partial, delayed custodian data

Custodians expose incomplete or lagging data. The UI had to stay honest about it, surfacing what's known, what's pending and what's stale, instead of pretending the picture was always complete.

If I did it again

Two things I'd change with hindsight.

Design the empty, error and stale-data states first, not last. Custodian data is partial and delayed, but I designed the happy path first and retrofitted the pending / stale / missing states. In a data product that's exactly where trust is won or lost, so next time they belong in the first design, not a cleanup pass.

Test the "answer for an auditor" job from day one. I led with real-time and added the historical view once feedback pushed me there, but it turned out to be half the product. I'd validate the reporting and audit job from the start rather than treating it as a follow-up.
